Date: 12 August 2026

Location: Herrera de Pisuerga Aerodrome, Palencia, Spain

The Equipment I Used

For this eclipse, I wanted not only to observe the event, but also to explore some simple optical methods for observing the Sun indirectly. I therefore built projection systems using everyday materials and experimented with small apertures to form images of the solar disk.

The optical principle. A small opening can act as a pinhole. Light from different points of the Sun travels through the opening in different directions and forms an image on a surface behind it.

During a solar eclipse, the projected image can reproduce the changing apparent shape of the solar disk as the Moon moves across the Sun. This means that the projected pattern is not simply a shadow: it is an optical image produced by the geometry of the incoming sunlight.

Homemade Projection Experiments

I wanted to explore the eclipse using simple optical experiments made from everyday materials. Instead of observing the Sun directly, I used small openings to project its image onto a white surface.

1. A Homemade Pinhole Camera

I used a Pringles tube to create a simple camera obscura. The small aperture allowed sunlight to enter the tube and form an image of the Sun on the projection surface.

The physics: Light travels approximately in straight lines. When the aperture is sufficiently small, rays coming from different points of the Sun can form an image on the opposite surface.

The image is inverted because rays from the upper part of the Sun cross the rays from the lower part as they pass through the aperture.

2. Projecting the Sun Through a Colander

I then used a fruit colander with many small openings. Each opening produced its own projection of the Sun, creating a pattern of multiple solar images.

Why are there many Suns? Each opening acts approximately as an independent pinhole. Because the Sun is an extended light source rather than a point source, each opening can form an image of the solar disk.

During an eclipse, these projected images reproduce the changing shape of the partially covered solar disk.

3. Projecting the Eclipse

I projected the Sun onto a white cardboard screen and was able to follow the changing shape of the solar disk as the Moon moved across it.

An important observation: the projected image is not simply the shadow of the Moon. It is an image of the Sun itself, formed by the geometry of the light passing through the small aperture.

As the Moon covered an increasing fraction of the solar disk, the shape of the projected image changed accordingly.

4. Continuing the Projection Experiment: The Skimmer

I continued the same pinhole projection experiment using a kitchen skimmer. Instead of one aperture, the skimmer contains many small openings, allowing multiple solar images to be formed simultaneously.

The physical phenomenon: pinhole projection. Each small opening of the skimmer acts approximately as an independent pinhole. Because the Sun has a finite angular size, light arriving from different points of the solar disk travels in slightly different directions.

Each opening therefore forms its own image of the Sun. Since the skimmer contains many openings, many solar images appear simultaneously on the projection surface.

The skimmer therefore acts as an array of simple pinhole projectors. The same physical principle connects this experiment with the homemade camera obscura made from the Pringles tube.

What I learned from these experiments.

These experiments showed that sophisticated optical equipment is not always necessary to demonstrate fundamental principles of optics. With a small aperture, a projection surface and sunlight, it is possible to observe the changing geometry of the solar disk during an eclipse.

Robotic Telescope Observation

During the eclipse, I also had the opportunity to observe the Sun remotely through a robotic telescope operated by a friend.

Using a VPN connection, I accessed the telescope system remotely and viewed its live solar image on my mobile phone. I took screenshots of what I was seeing on the screen, capturing both the solar disk and a visible sunspot.

Observing a Sunspot

The dark feature visible on the solar disk is a sunspot: a relatively cooler region of the Sun's photosphere associated with a strong concentration of magnetic fields. It appears darker than its surroundings because the region is cooler than the surrounding photosphere, although it is still extremely hot.

This observation was especially interesting because it allowed me to see a real solar feature using a telescope located remotely, turning my mobile phone into a window onto an astronomical instrument operating at a different location.
