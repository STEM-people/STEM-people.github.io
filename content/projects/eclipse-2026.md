# 12 August 2026 — Total Solar Eclipse

## Observation

**Date:** 12 August 2026

**Location:** Herrera de Pisuerga Aerodrome, Palencia, Spain

![Total solar eclipse at maximum, photographed with my mobile phone, with Venus visible in the upper left](/astronomy/eclipse-2026/media/EclipseConVenus-2.jpg "Totality at maximum eclipse, photographed with my mobile phone. Venus is visible in the upper left.")

## The Equipment I Used

For this eclipse, I wanted not only to observe the event, but also to explore
some simple optical methods for observing the Sun indirectly. I therefore built
projection systems using everyday materials and experimented with small
apertures to form images of the solar disk.

![Materials prepared for the homemade solar projection experiments](/astronomy/eclipse-2026/media/ParteMaterialUsado-boteFruteroCartulina.jpg "Materials prepared for the homemade solar projection experiments. Cardboard, paper, a cylindrical container and a perforated kitchen utensil were used to construct simple solar projection systems.")

![Perforated kitchen utensil used for solar projection](/astronomy/eclipse-2026/media/coladorAguaFruta.jpg "A perforated kitchen utensil used as a solar projection device. Its small openings can act approximately as pinholes, allowing the shape of the Sun to be projected onto a nearby surface.")

> **The optical principle.** A small opening can act as a pinhole. Light from
> different points of the Sun travels through the opening in different
> directions and forms an image on a surface behind it.
>
> During a solar eclipse, the projected image can reproduce the changing
> apparent shape of the solar disk as the Moon moves across the Sun. This means
> that the projected pattern is not simply a shadow: it is an optical image
> produced by the geometry of the incoming sunlight.

## Homemade Projection Experiments

I wanted to explore the eclipse using simple optical experiments made from
everyday materials. Instead of observing the Sun directly, I used small openings
to project its image onto a white surface.

### 1. A Homemade Pinhole Camera

![Homemade pinhole camera made from a Pringles tube](/astronomy/eclipse-2026/media/MontajeCamaraOscura.jpg "A homemade pinhole camera made from a Pringles tube. A small aperture was made in the tube, creating a simple camera obscura for projecting the Sun.")

I used a Pringles tube to create a simple camera obscura. The small aperture
allowed sunlight to enter the tube and form an image of the Sun on the
projection surface.

> **The physics:** light travels approximately in straight lines. When the
> aperture is sufficiently small, rays coming from different points of the Sun
> can form an image on the opposite surface.
>
> The image is inverted because rays from the upper part of the Sun cross the
> rays from the lower part as they pass through the aperture.

### 2. Projecting the Sun Through a Colander

![Solar projection through a perforated fruit colander](/astronomy/eclipse-2026/media/proyeccionColadorFruta.jpg "Multiple images of the Sun projected through the openings of a fruit colander.")

I then used a fruit colander with many small openings. Each opening produced its
own projection of the Sun, creating a pattern of multiple solar images.

> **Why are there many Suns?** Each opening acts approximately as an independent
> pinhole. Because the Sun is an extended light source rather than a point
> source, each opening can form an image of the solar disk.
>
> During an eclipse, these projected images reproduce the changing shape of the
> partially covered solar disk.

### 3. Projecting the Eclipse

![Partially eclipsed Sun projected onto white cardboard](/astronomy/eclipse-2026/media/ProyeccionPrimerContactoCartulina.jpg "The partially eclipsed Sun projected onto a white cardboard screen during the early phase of the eclipse.")

I projected the Sun onto a white cardboard screen and was able to follow the
changing shape of the solar disk as the Moon moved across it.

> **An important observation:** the projected image is not simply the shadow of
> the Moon. It is an image of the Sun itself, formed by the geometry of the
> light passing through the small aperture.
>
> As the Moon covered an increasing fraction of the solar disk, the shape of the
> projected image changed accordingly.

### 4. Continuing the Projection Experiment: The Skimmer

I continued the same pinhole projection experiment using a kitchen skimmer.
Instead of one aperture, the skimmer contains many small openings, allowing
multiple solar images to be formed simultaneously.

![Incoming sunlight reaching a kitchen skimmer during a solar projection experiment](/astronomy/eclipse-2026/media/ProyeccionEspumaderaAmpliada-FlechaEntrandoLuna.jpg "Incoming light — sunlight reaches the kitchen skimmer from the direction of the Sun. The small openings act approximately as individual pinholes through which the incoming light passes.")

![Outgoing light producing multiple solar images after passing through a kitchen skimmer](/astronomy/eclipse-2026/media/EspumaderaYlunaSaliendoDeEclipse-FlechaSaliente.jpg "Outgoing light — after passing through the openings, the light continues toward the projection surface. Each opening can produce its own image of the partially eclipsed Sun.")

The two photographs document the same optical process: sunlight enters the
apertures of the skimmer and the resulting light produces multiple images of the
solar disk on the projection surface.

> **The physical phenomenon: pinhole projection.** Each small opening of the
> skimmer acts approximately as an independent pinhole. Because the Sun has a
> finite angular size, light arriving from different points of the solar disk
> travels in slightly different directions.
>
> Each opening therefore forms its own image of the Sun. Since the skimmer
> contains many openings, many solar images appear simultaneously on the
> projection surface.
>
> The skimmer therefore acts as an array of simple pinhole projectors. The same
> physical principle connects this experiment with the homemade camera obscura
> made from the Pringles tube.

> **What I learned from these experiments.** Sophisticated optical equipment is
> not always necessary to demonstrate fundamental principles of optics. With a
> small aperture, a projection surface and sunlight, it is possible to observe
> the changing geometry of the solar disk during an eclipse.

## Robotic Telescope Observation

During the eclipse, I also had the opportunity to observe the Sun remotely
through a robotic telescope operated by a friend.

Using a VPN connection, I accessed the telescope system remotely and viewed its
live solar image on my mobile phone. I took screenshots of what I was seeing on
the screen, capturing both the solar disk and a visible sunspot.

![Two mobile phone screenshots showing the Sun and a sunspot observed remotely through a robotic telescope](/astronomy/eclipse-2026/media/MontajeCapturasManchaMovilTelescopioDaniel.jpg "Remote observation of the Sun through a robotic telescope. The two screenshots, taken from my mobile phone, show the solar disk and a visible sunspot.")

### Observing a Sunspot

The dark feature visible on the solar disk is a **sunspot**: a relatively cooler
region of the Sun's photosphere associated with a strong concentration of
magnetic fields. It appears darker than its surroundings because the region is
cooler than the surrounding photosphere, although it is still extremely hot.

This observation was especially interesting because it allowed me to see a real
solar feature using a telescope located remotely, turning my mobile phone into a
window onto an astronomical instrument operating at a different location.

## Scientific Context

### Why Can a Solar Eclipse Be Total? — The Geometrical Coincidence

The possibility of a total solar eclipse is related to the apparent angular size
of the Sun and the Moon as seen from Earth.

For relatively small angles, the apparent angular size can be approximated by:

$$ θ ≈ D / d $$

where **D** is the diameter of the object and **d** is its distance from the
observer.

![Handwritten explanation of the geometrical coincidence that makes a total solar eclipse possible](/astronomy/eclipse-2026/media/1.jpg)

![Handwritten numerical explanation comparing the physical sizes and distances of the Sun and Moon](/astronomy/eclipse-2026/media/2.jpg)

![Handwritten geometrical explanation using similar triangles to compare the apparent sizes of the Sun and Moon](/astronomy/eclipse-2026/media/3.jpg)

### The coincidence is not exact or permanent

The Moon's orbit is elliptical, so the Earth–Moon distance changes. The Earth is
also at different distances from the Sun during the year.

Therefore, sometimes the Moon can completely cover the solar disk, producing a
total solar eclipse, while at other times a ring of photosphere remains visible
around the Moon, producing an annular solar eclipse.
